When should local evidence change the recommendation?

Behavioral embeddings reveal what customers are likely to choose. Propensity neighborhoods ask a harder second question: is the nearby evidence strong enough to trust?

Research and paper by Jonathan R. Landers · September 2026

$\text{trust local evidence} \iff \text{match gain} > \text{support cost}$

The best recommendation is not always the most locally refined one. It is the one whose specificity is still supported by evidence.

The story arc

From behavioral signal to an honest decision.

Each step solves one problem and exposes the next. The empirical result gives the three steps distinct jobs.

Represent

Behavior creates the geometry.

Compress pre-decision activity into an embedding, combine it with ordinary customer attributes, and estimate enrollment propensity for every eligible program.

This is where the main predictive gain appears.

Refine

Specificity spends support.

Add matching dimensions to find more relevant peers. The remaining neighbors become more alike—but every refinement can only reduce their number.

A better match arrives with greater uncertainty.

Decide

The frontier governs trust.

Balance residual mismatch against finite-sample uncertainty. Correct when supported; otherwise keep the direct ranking or flag low confidence.

The theory makes restraint part of the method.

The mathematical core

One bound captures the trade.

The notation says something simple: local correction is useful only while closer peers compensate for having fewer of them.

The refinement frontier

At neighborhood level $m$, the score uncertainty is bounded by a mismatch term plus a support term:

\[ b_m(x)=\varepsilon_m^\rho(x)+B\sqrt{\frac{\log(2K_x/\alpha)}{2n_m}}. \]

Under the theorem's assumptions, the chosen program's propensity regret is at most $2b_m(x)$. The preferred level minimizes the frontier rather than blindly choosing the most detailed neighborhood.

$\varepsilon_m^\rho(x)$ · local mismatchHow different the remaining model errors may be among nearby customers.
$n_m$ · peer supportHow many reference customers remain after level $m$ of refinement.
$K_x$ · eligible choicesHow many programs compete for this customer's recommendation.

Conceptual frontier

Refinement is not free. Mismatch can fall as the peer definition tightens. The sampling cost rises as support disappears. Their sum determines how much local evidence deserves to influence the decision.

Chronological KuaiRec study

The representation wins. The neighborhood calibrates.

The strongest result is also the simplest operational recommendation: learn from behavior, then rank eligible programs directly by fitted propensity. KuaiRec is an almost fully observed dataset of viewing and feedback logs from the Kuaishou video app.

Propensity RMSE−31.9%Behavioral embedding versus static features
Top-1 regret−6.4%Lower regret across the prespecified comparison
Exact hit rate+2.58 ppImproved in nine of ten chronological splits

The honest ending: neighborhoods contain real calibration information, but neither they nor the supplemental reranking variants establish a confirmed improvement in the winning recommendation. Better probability estimates do not automatically produce a better top choice.
